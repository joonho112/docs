Mapping Child Care Deserts in Alabama: A Methodological Comparison

JoonHo Lee and Alison Hooper · July 2026

Abstract

Child care desert maps increasingly guide state and federal investments, yet the communities a map designates as underserved depend on how spatial access is measured. We compared three accessibility measures: the contained provider-to-population ratio (PPR), the two-step floating catchment area (2SFCA), and its enhanced, distance-decayed extension (E2SFCA). All three were computed from statewide administrative licensing records covering 2,164 providers and 117,062 licensed slots, American Community Survey estimates of 294,417 children under age 5, and road-network travel times from census tract population centers to providers in Alabama. Statewide licensed capacity amounted to 0.398 slots per child. Under a common desert threshold of 0.33 slots per child applied to the same 1,409 positive-demand tracts, PPR classified 875 tracts (62.1%) as deserts, 2SFCA 647 (45.9%), and E2SFCA 690 (49.0%); PPR and E2SFCA agreed only moderately (kappa = 0.45). Discordant tracts revealed distinct mechanisms: 287 PPR-only desert tracts, mostly urban, averaged more than 3,200 licensed slots within a 15-minute drive, whereas 102 E2SFCA-only desert tracts reflected competition for nearby capacity. Desert status was strongly rural (72.2% of rural vs. 17.0% of urban tracts; risk ratio 4.24) and stable for some tracts but definition-sensitive for others; 519 tracts remained deserts across thresholds from 0.20 to 0.50, while 370 changed classification. Community socioeconomic characteristics added descriptive information beyond geography but supported no causal interpretation. We recommend reporting measurement-robust and definition-sensitive desert designations together and discuss implications for research and state policy targeting.
