Where the Children Are: Household-Informed versus Areal Demand and the Child Care Desert Map

JoonHo Lee and Alison Hooper · July 2026

Abstract

Maps of child care deserts, communities where licensed care is scarce relative to the number of young children, have become a common way of describing where the supply of early care and education falls short. Most such maps represent demand in the same way, assuming that every child in a census tract lives at one representative point. We examine how much this convention matters by rebuilding Alabama’s desert map with demand placed where households live. A commercial residence file covering 1.8 million households provides the locations, aggregated to a privacy-protected grid, and American Community Survey estimates provide the number of young children. Supply, travel times, the accessibility model, and the desert threshold are held fixed, so that any change in the map reflects demand placement alone. The statewide share of children classified as living in a desert is nearly unchanged, moving by about one percentage point. In contrast, 137 of 1,409 tracts, about one in ten, change desert status, in both directions, and the tracts that change are identifiable in advance from the mismatch between travel times measured at the tract center and those its households face. A routine choice of distance-decay specification moves the statewide rate several times more than demand placement does. We conclude that areal demand is a reasonable approximation for statewide totals, that local designations deserve a sensitivity check before they guide investment, and that commercial residence data offer a practical way of providing one.
