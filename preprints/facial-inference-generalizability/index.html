Signal, State, or Sensor Style? Generalizability and Interpretive Limits of Automated Facial-Inference Channels During Cognitive Assessment

JoonHo Lee and Yurou Wang · August 2026

Abstract

Commercial facial-expression software can be attached to a computer-administered cognitive assessment so that, while a student works through the items, the software logs a dense stream of emotion-labeled output. Such channels are increasingly analyzed as measures of emotion during testing, and their volume, often hundreds of thousands of time-stamped records in a modest study, invites the conclusion that they must be measuring something well. That conclusion conflates three properties requiring separate evidence: how much was recorded, whether the resulting scores distinguish one person from another consistently, and whether they add information about outcomes of substantive interest. We separate the three properties in archival data from a 25-item untimed reasoning task in which 169 college students, tested partly in person and partly over Zoom after a COVID-19 interruption, were monitored by a commercial system emitting nine facial-inference channels. Since adjacent observations of the same face are strongly autocorrelated, we evaluate repeatability under deliberately conservative accountings of the information the stream contains and corroborate the model-based results with model-free item splits. Eight of the nine activation channels order persons consistently even under the harshest within-cell accounting; the sparse Joy channel is the instructive exception. The channels also share a common component, but recording volume and shared software processing remain rival explanations for it. Despite this repeatability, the evaluated channel scores improved held-out prediction of no behavioral or survey benchmark. Repeatability and usefulness therefore required separate evidence, and we summarize the workflow as reporting guidance that keeps observation volume, repeatability, and utility distinct.
