How Far Do Subsidized Families Travel to Child Care? Excess Commuting in Alabama’s Child Care Subsidy System

JoonHo Lee and Alison Hooper · July 2026

Abstract

Families who receive child care subsidies often travel past providers close to home, and the administrative records that document their enrollment say little about why. We apply the excess-commuting framework to Alabama’s subsidy system, comparing the travel that 38,069 subsidized children undertake with the minimum that the locations, licensed capacities, and age restrictions of participating providers would permit. Every observed home-to-provider trip is routed over the road network, and the minimum is obtained by solving capacity-constrained transportation problems. Observed one-way travel averages 13.4 minutes against a constrained minimum of 5.4 minutes, so about 60% of observed travel time is in excess of what the spatial configuration of supply would require. The estimate changes little when providers along commuting corridors are treated as nearly costless detours, when origins are shifted from census tracts to block groups, and when demand is repeatedly resampled. Contrary to what a rural-deficit account would predict, the excess among participating families is largest for infants and in metropolitan Birmingham rather than in the rural Black Belt. Revealed use of providers declines exponentially with travel time, with a half-life of about 6.5 minutes, but retains appreciable weight well beyond the 15-minute catchments that accessibility studies commonly impose. We argue that excess commuting should be read as a diagnostic of unrealized proximity rather than as evidence that reassigning children toward the minimum would improve welfare, and we discuss what the age and geographic patterns imply for subsidy administration and for catchment conventions in accessibility measurement.
