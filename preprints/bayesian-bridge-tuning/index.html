Designing Experiments to Predict Unit-Specific Treatment Effects: A Bayesian Bridge with Implementable Tuning

JoonHo Lee · August 2026

Abstract

Randomized experiments are typically designed to detect an average treatment effect, but their results are increasingly used to predict treatment effects for individual units. Tipton and Mamakos made such prediction an explicit design goal, evaluating sample size, moderator count, and site selection by the mean squared error of the predicted unit-specific effects. We develop a Bayesian decision-theoretic counterpart to their calculus and show that the two agree: under diffuse priors, the design-stage posterior-predictive criterion recovers their benchmark to first order, with exact finite-sample and cross-population corrections showing where the first-order arithmetic understates the risk. The Bayesian form makes several design levers explicit, including prior evidence from related studies, a continuum of analyses between the average-effect and fully moderated endpoints, the match between the experimental sample and the prediction target, and the correlation between potential outcomes, which no experiment can identify and which determines whether a stated accuracy target is attainable. The continuum raises a tuning problem that the design criterion cannot settle, because averaged under its own prior it always favors more shrinkage. Transferring classical shrinkage theory resolves it, with conditions attached: with three or more moderators, positive-part shrinkage protects against the moderated analysis’s estimation cost at a bounded price; no data-driven rule can protect both endpoints; and pretesting for moderation performs worst precisely where the moderation question is genuinely uncertain. A single matrix, computable at the design stage, prices the prediction burden of a mismatched target and decides whether a chosen tuning rule keeps its guarantee.
